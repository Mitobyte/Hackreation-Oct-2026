Executive summary

Written in ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English: short sentences, active voice, one idea per sentence.

Recommendation: Build one app that runs on a MacBook. It fills ERI's Word templates from exported data. It writes a resume and cover letter from a client's bio. It writes a job ad from a business's tasks. Then it matches ads to people. Staff spend about half their time on paperwork, so this saves the most time. A separate reviewer ranked these four parts highest of 24 options.

Who ERI is. ERI is a nonprofit in Madison, Wisconsin. It started in 1990. It helps people with disabilities find work, keep work, and keep their benefits.

Who ERI serves. ERI serves three groups:

  1. People with disabilities. Many get SSI or SSDI. Some are 18 to 21 and leave school soon.

  2. Employers that want to hire these people.

  3. Agencies and schools that train staff. ERI makes websites and courses for them.

How ERI adds value. ERI gets a referral from the state. ERI has two separate services: benefits counseling and employment. Sometimes one intake starts both. In employment, staff find the job (job development). A job coach supports the person at the job site. Then the coach fades out. In benefits, expert counselors decide if a person can safely take more hours or a raise.

The main fear. Many people fear that work will stop their cash benefits or health care. This fear stops them before they apply. ERI is the expert in this area in Wisconsin.

The limits. Client data contains health data. The law (HIPAA) and government contracts protect it. DVR pays ERI only when a person gets an outcome, such as a job. Staff must send reports all the time. Social Security takes weeks or months to verify benefits. There is probably no money to maintain a new tool.

SWOT in four lines.

  • Strength: 35 years of trust and benefits expertise.

  • Weakness: much manual work and data in many places.

  • Opportunity: give employers a simple way to make good jobs.

  • Threat: cuts to government funds and privacy risk.

The four problems are two pairs. Problems 1 and 4 are about getting a job. Problems 2 and 3 are about keeping a job. Each pair is one process, seen from two sides.

Problems ERI did not name. The most important one is the benefits question: "What happens to my benefits if I work this many hours?" Another one is that ERI's job-match knowledge is in a database that staff cannot easily search.

Who ERI is and who they serve

ERI (Employment Resources Inc.) is a nonprofit in Madison, Wisconsin. It started in 1990. It helps people with disabilities get work, keep work, and keep their benefits. (About)

ERI has three service lines:

The core insight (since 1992): fear of losing cash benefits and health care stops many people from working. ERI built its brand on benefits counseling to remove that fear. ERI is now the only WIPA provider in Wisconsin. ERI PLUS serves Ticket to Work clients in Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Minnesota, Ohio, and mostly Wisconsin.

People ERI serves (from the session):

  • Adults on SSI or SSDI who want to start work, return to work, or work more hours.

  • Youth aged 18 to 21 in the transition program. They often have higher barriers and need customized employment. Some can work 20 hours a week; some can work only 5.

  • People who never worked. Many went to school, then stayed at home.

  • Employers who want to hire but do not know how.

Value chain

Benefits counseling and employment are two separate services. One intake can start one or both. Job development (finding the job) is where P1, P4, and H2 meet. Benefits experts, not software, decide whether a person can safely take more hours or a raise.

ERI counts success when the coach has faded and the person keeps the job alone. A raise or more hours sends the case back to the benefits expert. Reporting runs under every step, and DVR pays only at milestones.

SWOT

ERI's biggest strength is trust and benefits expertise. Its biggest weakness is manual, scattered data. Its biggest opportunity is to turn 35 years of job-match knowledge into a tool that employers use.

Strengths (internal, helpful)

  • 35 years of trust with SSA, DVR, DHS, and counties.

  • Sole WIPA provider in Wisconsin. Top-20 national Ticket to Work network (2011–2013).

  • In-house web, design, and LMS team. This is rare for a nonprofit.

  • National partners: GWU, VRTAC-QE, NTACT:C.

  • A Salesforce database of employers, jobs, and placed people.

  • Person-centered culture. Candid Gold transparency seal.

Weaknesses (internal, harmful)

  • Much work is manual: staff keep rules, records, and portals up to date by hand.

  • Reporting is constant and eats staff time.

  • DVR pays only for outcomes, so unpaid hours rise when a client takes longer.

  • Data is split across Salesforce, local databases, and state portals. Salesforce does not fit disability services, so staff work around it.

  • Revenue depends on government contracts and fee-for-service. There is little money to maintain new tools.

  • Job coaches are spread thin across worksites.

  • The employer-side offer is less developed than the client-side offer.

  • Outcome data (fading, tenure, wage growth) is hard to show.

Opportunities (external, helpful)

  • Employers need workers. Disability-inclusive firms report better results (Accenture 2023).

  • Other employers could copy Kwik Trip-style roles.

  • AI can quickly draft task guides, job breakdowns, and skill profiles.

  • Remote coaching can reach rural sites. ERI already hires in Ashland.

  • The design team could package tools and sell them to other providers.

Threats (external, harmful)

  • Changes to Medicaid, FoodShare work rules, and community living rules (ERI news).

  • Federal grant cuts to WIPA or technical assistance centers.

  • Government shutdowns slow Social Security, so benefits checks take longer.

  • Privacy breach risk with health and benefits data.

  • Vendor lock-in (Salesforce, Setworks).

  • Staff shortage and turnover in direct support jobs.

  • Clients can lose benefits if work is not planned well.

Market and competition

The need is large, and funding is under pressure. No current tool joins these steps into one workflow. P3 (asking for help) has the fewest existing tools.

Market facts

  • The jobs gap: in 2025, 22.8% of people with a disability had a job, against 65.2% of people without one. About 30% of workers with a disability work part time (BLS).

  • The state budget squeeze: Wisconsin DVR started a waitlist (order of selection) in December 2025 after a $4.6M shortfall. Funding was approved in June 2026, and DVR aims to end the waitlist by June 30, 2027 (DWD, OOS FAQ).

  • Placements in Wisconsin: in SFY2025, DVR placed 3,166 people in competitive integrated employment at a median of $15/hour and 20 hours/week. Long-term care placed 7,976 people at a median of $12/hour and 12 hours/week. The barriers named were provider capacity, turnover, and wrong beliefs about benefits (Act 178 report).

  • Staff shortage: turnover among Wisconsin direct support staff was 41.7% in 2023, with a $14.37 average starting wage (DHS). Tools that save coach time matter.

  • The 2026 benefits cliff numbers: SGA is $1,690/month (non-blind), a trial work month is $1,210, the SSI federal rate is $994/month, and the student earned income exclusion is up to $2,410/month for people under 22 (SSA Red Book). The student exclusion matters for ERI's 18–21 clients.

  • New paperwork risk: the FoodShare work rules exempt people who cannot work for health reasons, but they must claim the exemption themselves (DHS). The BadgerCare work rules start in 2027, and SSI and disability-waiver enrollees are exempt (DHS).

Problem statements and personas

ERI gave four problem statements. Each one has a main persona and a second persona on the other side of the problem. The personas are composites made from the session notes; they are not real clients.

P1: Resumes hide real ability

ERI's words: Traditional resumes may not show a person's abilities, especially with little work history, communication disabilities, or trouble writing about strengths. How can we help employers recognize people's abilities rather than focus on traditional qualifications?

Main persona: Marcus, 19, transition program. Marcus finished high school and stayed home for a year. He has never had a paid job. He is great at sorting and fixing bikes, but he freezes in interviews and writes in short phrases. His one-page resume says "no experience." Employers skip it.

  • Goal: Get a job working with his hands, about 10 hours a week.

  • Pain: Paper hides what he can do; interviews test talking, not doing.

  • Quote: "I can show you. I just can't write it."

Second persona: Priya, hiring manager. She scans 60 applications a week. She has no time to decode a sparse resume.

HMW: How might we show a job seeker's demonstrated skills to employers, for people with little work history, so that employers invite them to a working interview?

P2: Instructions people can use alone

ERI's words: Some employees need support to remember task sequences, follow instructions, move between activities, or handle changes. Printed checklists or verbal instructions may not meet each person's needs. How can we make workplace instructions easier to understand and use so employees can work more independently?

Main persona: Dani, 24, stocker at a grocery store. Dani is autistic. She does the morning stock run well. When the manager changes the order of aisles, she loses her place and stops working.

  • Goal: Do the full shift without a coach next to her.

  • Pain: Steps live in the coach's head. The paper list does not change when the day changes.

  • Quote: "Just tell me what's next."

Second persona: Tom, job coach. He writes task lists by hand in Word for each client and each site. When a task changes, he drives back to retrain.

HMW: How might we let coaches and employers make step-by-step task guides (picture, audio, or text) for each worker so that the worker finishes a full shift without in-person prompts?

P3: Asking for help without a coach on site

ERI's words: Employees sometimes need help with tasks, communication, changed routines, or surprises. Asking can be hard because of communication disabilities, not knowing whom to contact, or discomfort interrupting a supervisor. Coaches support several people at different sites. How can technology make it easier to ask for and get support without unnecessary dependence on a job coach?

Main persona: Leo, 31, dishwasher. Leo uses few spoken words and a text-to-speech app. When the dish machine breaks, he does not know whom to tell. He waits, and the shift lead thinks that he is idle.

  • Goal: Fix small problems himself; get the right person fast for big ones.

  • Pain: No simple way to say "I'm stuck" to the right person.

Second persona: Tasha, job coach with 9 clients at 6 sites. Her phone rings all day. She cannot tell which calls are urgent. She wants each client to need her less over time.

HMW: How might we give workers a one-tap way to say what they need, routed to self-help first, then a coworker, then the coach, so that coach visits go down month over month?

P4: Employers want to hire but lack a playbook

ERI's words: Employers may want to hire people with disabilities but do not know how to match skills to business needs, design job duties, respond to disclosure, handle accommodation requests, or onboard. JAN has good information but no simple personalized workflow. How can technology help employers move from recognizing skills to creating meaningful jobs and supporting success at work?

Main persona: Greg, operations manager at a 120-person Wisconsin manufacturer. Greg has 8 open roles. He likes the idea of hiring through ERI. He does not know how to split work into a role, what to say if a worker discloses a disability, or how to answer an accommodation request legally.

  • Goal: Fill real work gaps with people who stay.

  • Pain: Fear of a legal mistake; no time to read JAN articles.

  • Quote: "Tell me what job to create and what to do on day one."

Second persona: ERI employment specialist. She does this job-carving work by hand, one employer at a time. Kwik Trip did it well, but most employers do not know how to start.

HMW: How might we turn an employer's task list into ready-to-post carved roles plus an onboarding and accommodation checklist, for small and mid-size Wisconsin employers, so that they create a role within one week?

Which problems to tackle

Recommendation: build P1 + P4 as one two-sided product, a "skills-to-role" bridge. It fits the hackathon theme (connect people to jobs) most directly. It uses ERI's own job and placement data. It makes the employer offer stronger, which is ERI's weakest service line. Strong backup: P2 + P3 as one on-the-job support tool. It is easier to demo and has lower privacy risk.

What ERI's answers change (Oct 31):

  • Employers ask three things: how many hours, what exactly the person will do, and "do I have the capacity to teach this without 1:1 support?" A P4 role card must answer all three. The third answer is a P2 task guide.

  • One task video can do three jobs. Employers would record task videos (with releases). Families would agree to videos of the person doing tasks, shared by QR code. The same video shows skill (P1), defines the role (P4), and teaches the steps (P2). This joins all three problems at low cost.

  • Success means fading. ERI measures whether the coach has stepped back, whether the person works alone, and how long they keep the job. Faster fading also helps under DVR's outcome-only pay.

  • Keep it small. There is probably no money for upkeep, and ERI's in-house team works in PHP. Prefer a simple web app that the PHP team can host, with no paid AI service in the core flow.

  • Reports stay with Setworks. Do not build reporting. Export clean data that Setworks or Salesforce can take in.

The four official problems form two pairs. Each pair is one journey seen from two sides:

  • P1 + P4 = getting hired. P1 is the seeker showing ability. P4 is the employer turning that ability into a role. A skill profile from P1 is the input that P4 needs.

  • P2 + P3 = staying hired. P2 prevents the need for help (clear steps). P3 handles what P2 cannot prevent (ask for help). One app can do both: steps first, then a "stuck" button.

Questions to grill Setworks with

ERI says Salesforce "doesn't serve the people that we serve" and staff are "constantly on a hamster wheel just trying to operate in a system that's not meant to serve us." Setworks promises to handle reporting and case-management notes. Make Setworks prove that it cuts staff hours on ERI's real reports, not on a generic demo. Ask them to show each answer live, with ERI's own report forms.

Make them do these tasks live in the demo

  • Produce ERI's actual Wisconsin DVR milestone report and invoice from that client's notes. No retyping.

  • Produce one county report and one grant report from the same data. Show that staff enter each fact once.

  • Log a job coach visit from a phone at a worksite in under 2 minutes, then show it in the DVR report.

  • Show a benefits counselor's case: a pending SSA verification that has waited 6 weeks. How does the system track it and remind staff?

Harm risks the reviewer flagged

  • #21: a wrong column mapping puts one client's data in another client's document. Filled files on a laptop must be encrypted.

  • #22: a model may invent skills or overwrite the client's own voice. The client must review every letter.

  • #23: a model may carve duties that overload the person, or skip wage-law and accommodation checks. The employer must not treat the draft as final.

  • #24: a ranking can override client preference or score people low for having fewer listed skills. Show it as a suggestion, never a verdict.

  • #1 and #3: videos need releases, a way to revoke them, and secure links.

  • #9 and #10: wrong benefits information could make someone refuse a raise or lose SSI or Medicaid.

Top pick: the local paperwork and match pipeline

Recommendation: build #21–#24 as one Mac app that runs locally, funded first by ERI's employer consulting. One engine (CSV in, Word template out) does the paperwork, and it also writes the resume, cover letter, and job ad. A matcher then pairs ads with people. Client data stays on the Mac. The earlier bundle (Role Card, Step Cards, Fade Meter) stays as phase 2.

Setworks overlap (audit, Oct 31): Setworks can merge a client's form data into Word or Excel templates, one form at a time. So #21's edge is bulk runs, CSV input, and templates outside Setworks. Setworks shows no resume writer, job ad writer, or skills-to-tasks matcher, so #22–#24 stay open. See the Setworks Coverage report.

The flow has four steps:

  1. Autofill (#21): staff export a CSV from Salesforce or Setworks, pick a Word template, and get one filled document per client.

  2. Resume and cover letter (#22): the same engine takes a client's bio, interests, and skills, and a local model drafts the text. Staff and the client edit it.

  3. Job ad (#23): a business description plus easy tasks becomes a job ad and a carved role card (hours, duties, supports).

  4. Match (#24): the app scores each ad against each profile on tasks, hours, bus access, interests, and supports. It shows the top matches with reasons. Staff and the client decide.

Value created vs. value captured

  • Hard savings: staff hours on paperwork (about half their time today); faster job development, which reaches DVR milestones sooner; no new server or license.

  • Soft savings: employers get a clear ad and role; clients get a resume that shows ability; staff are less burned out; data stays on ERI's machine.
